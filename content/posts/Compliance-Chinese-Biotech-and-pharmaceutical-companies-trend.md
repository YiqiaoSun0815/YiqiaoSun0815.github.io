Recently, I’ve been onboarding more Chinese biotech, pharmaceutical and medical device companies that are setting up Swiss entities as part of their international expansion.

What I initially expected was a relatively straightforward “China company opening overseas subsidiary” structure.

But after seeing tens of these cases, I realized the logic is much deeper than that.

First of all,in many situations, the China entity gradually becomes more like a R&D or manufacturing service provider, while the Swiss entity starts taking over the more “international-facing” functions:
IP ownership, licensing activities, overseas fundraising, commercial cooperation and sometimes even treasury functions.

The share structure of these companies often involves complex equity tools such as trust,fundation,employee equity ownership plan,and they are usually invested by a HongKong SPV.I liaised with the UBOs,they tend to think HK SPV as a capital bridge of China and Switzerland.Due to the fast money-burning,these companies often bring in multiple rounds of investors which makes the share structure complex.

From onboarding perspective,these cased are much more complex and unusual,but it would be clear once I understand the business rantional behind it:it is increasingly about making the corporate structure, IP ownership and investor-facing setup understandable and acceptable to international banks, investors and counterparties.

Secondly,I’ve been thinking about is that this trend seems very different from the previous generation of “China outbound” businesses.

In traditional trading or manufacturing businesses, overseas entities were often mainly used for sales, logistics or tax efficiency.

But in biotech and advanced technology sectors, what companies are moving first is often:

IP
financing capability
international legal structure
global partnership access

while keeping a significant portion of the actual scientific and operational capability in China.

I increasingly feel that this reflects a broader shift:
global investors and institutions still want access to Chinese R&D capability,manufacturing efficiency and lower cost, but at the same time, they are becoming more sensitive to geopolitical, regulatory and structural risks.

And somewhere in between, jurisdictions like Switzerland are gradually becoming an important bridge layer between the two worlds.
